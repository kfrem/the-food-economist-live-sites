## WHAT CHANGED FROM VERSION 3, AND WHY

Version 3 described a single subscription at £299 a month, sold by walking into restaurants, delivered by hand. Three things about that were wrong, and this version corrects them.

**The positioning was left implicit.** The service is food economics consultancy: analysing a client's own operating information to advise on cost control, supplier sourcing, menu positioning and pricing. It is not bookkeeping and it is not the preparation of accounts. That distinction has to be visible in the language, the deliverables and the contract, and Section 2 sets out how.

**The price was wrong in shape, not only in level.** A single price cannot work across a café taking £5,500 a week and a restaurant taking £14,000. The arithmetic in Section 4 shows why: at a realistic realised saving of two per cent of turnover, the fee a small site can justify is about £160 a month, while a larger site can carry £350. One price either prices out the small sites or leaves money on the table with the large ones. The answer is a ladder, not a number.

**The business had only one revenue line.** A monthly subscription is the hardest thing to sell to a restaurant owner and the slowest to build. Meanwhile the founder holds an MSc in food economics, thirty years of finance experience and the ability to build software, and every one of those can be sold as a one-off with no ongoing labour attached. Version 4 puts four revenue lines to work at once, three of which produce cash inside thirty days.

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Restaurant Vitals is a food economics practice serving independent UK food businesses. It analyses a client's own operating information — supplier invoices, till summaries, rota costs, delivery platform statements — and advises the owner on what to control, where to buy, how to price and what to change this week.

The proposition rests on a gap that is real and unserved. Accountants describe the past for compliance. Till systems describe sales. Nobody stands between the two and tells an owner, in operating terms, that his fish is costing eleven per cent more than the market, that his Tuesday rota is over-staffed by one shift, or that forty per cent of his delivery orders are repeat customers who could be moved off commission.

The practice reaches the market through free public tools that any owner can use in sixty seconds, converts through low-cost one-off diagnostics, and retains through a three-tier advisory subscription. Every element is built by the founder using free-tier infrastructure. Total cash requirement in year one is under £250.

**Year one, on the assumptions in Section 5:**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Revenue | £59,849 |
| Cash costs | £2,400 |
| Cash available to founder | £57,449 |
| Month 12 recurring revenue | £5,375 a month |
| Month 12 total run rate | £11,704 a month |
| Cash required to start | Under £250 |

The binding constraint is not money and it is not demand. It is founder hours, and the model hits that wall in month eight. Section 6 sets out exactly what must be automated before then, and the companion coding plan builds it.

## 2. POSITIONING — WHAT THIS PRACTICE IS

### 2.1 The professional frame

This is a **food economics and operational consultancy**. The founder analyses client operating information in order to give commercial advice: cost control, procurement, supplier selection, menu engineering, pricing, channel mix and capacity planning. That is consultancy work grounded in an MSc in Food Economics and Marketing and thirty years of commercial finance experience.

It is not the preparation of accounts, the maintenance of statutory records, the filing of returns, or the provision of tax advice. The client keeps his own bookkeeper and his own accountant, and Restaurant Vitals never displaces either. Where the analysis touches the client's financial records, it does so as an input to commercial advice, in the same way a procurement consultant reads purchase invoices or a yield consultant reads a rate card.

### 2.2 Language discipline

The positioning holds only if the vocabulary holds. Every page of the website, every proposal and every client message uses the left column and never the right.

| Use this | Never this |
|---|---|
| Operating review, cost review | Management accounts |
| Cost analysis, variance review | Bookkeeping, data entry |
| Advisory note, action note | Report, financial statement |
| Cost lines, operating lines | Nominal codes, ledger, chart of accounts |
| Your figures, your operating data | Your books, your records |
| Food economist, operations adviser | Accountant, bookkeeper, finance director |
| Analysis pack for your own accountant | Export pack, Xero-ready file |
| Advisory engagement, consultancy retainer | Bookkeeping service, outsourced finance |

Two practical consequences follow. **Do not offer a downloadable file formatted for accounting software** — that single deliverable does more to blur the line than anything else in Version 3. Offer a commercial summary instead: cost movements, supplier price changes, procurement recommendations. If the client's accountant wants transaction data, the client's own bookkeeper supplies it.

**Do not maintain records as a service.** Analyse them, advise on them, hand back the analysis. The client's own systems remain the record.

### 2.3 What this frees, and what it does not

The consultancy frame removes the accountancy supervision question from the critical path. It does not remove three obligations that apply to any consultancy handling client business data, and two of these are already settled:

- **ICO registration** — secured.
- **Professional indemnity insurance** — a consultant advising on procurement and pricing carries advice risk. Cover for a sole practitioner at this turnover runs to roughly £300 to £700 a year and can be bought monthly. This is the one thing worth spending money on before the first paid engagement.
- **Terms of engagement** — scope, limitation of liability, and an explicit statement that the work is commercial advice and not accountancy, audit or tax advice. That statement protects the positioning as much as it protects the founder. Section 9 gives the wording.

## 3. THE MARKET AND THE ENTRY POINT

### 3.1 Who the client is

Independent, owner-operated, single-site or two-site food businesses in Greater London: cafés, fish and chip shops, pubs with kitchens, takeaways, pizzerias, all-day diners and full restaurants. Weekly turnover between £4,000 and £15,000. No finance function. An accountant seen once or twice a year. A till system that reports sales and nothing else.

### 3.2 The four things they actually feel

Owners do not wake up wanting financial insight. They wake up with four specific pains, and the practice sells against these and not against abstractions:

1. **"My food bill keeps going up and I do not know why."** Supplier price drift is invisible without line-level comparison across weeks. This is pure food economics and it is the founder's strongest ground.
2. **"The delivery platforms are eating me alive."** Commission at twenty-eight to thirty-two per cent on a growing share of covers, with no visibility of the true contribution per channel.
3. **"I am busy but there is no money at the end of the month."** Volume without margin. Menu mix, portion drift, waste.
4. **"I cannot tell if I am overstaffed until it is too late."** Rota cost against takings by hour and by day.

Each of these maps to a free public tool, and each free tool maps to a paid product. That is the whole funnel.

### 3.3 Why the free tools are the strategy, not the marketing

An owner will not book a meeting with a consultant he has never heard of. He will type a number into a calculator on his phone while the fryers heat up. The tools do four jobs at once:

- They demonstrate competence before any conversation happens.
- They rank in search. "Restaurant food cost percentage calculator", "Deliveroo commission calculator UK", "menu GP calculator" are queries with real, continuous volume and almost no serious UK competition.
- They qualify. A visitor who enters £12,000 weekly takings and a thirty-six per cent food cost has identified himself as a client and told you his problem before you have spoken.
- They cost nothing to run. Static pages, no servers, no per-user cost.

This replaces the fifty walk-ins a month in Version 3, which required roughly one hundred visits to add three clients. The tools work while the founder sleeps and while he is delivering for existing clients.

## 4. PRICING — REBUILT FROM THE CLIENT'S MARGIN

### 4.1 The affordability arithmetic

You were right that £299 is hard to justify, and here is the proof in numbers. Independent food businesses run at roughly four to six per cent net.

| Weekly takings | Annual turnover | Net at 4–6% | £99/mo as % of net | £249/mo | £449/mo |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| £5,500 | £286,000 | £11,440 | 10.4% | 26.1% | 47.1% |
| £7,000 | £364,000 | £18,200 | 6.5% | 16.4% | 29.6% |
| £9,000 | £468,000 | £23,400 | 5.1% | 12.8% | 23.0% |
| £12,000 | £624,000 | £37,440 | 3.2% | 8.0% | 14.4% |
| £14,000 | £728,000 | £43,680 | 2.7% | 6.8% | 12.3% |

A £449 fee to a £5,500-a-week café is forty-seven per cent of everything the owner takes home. He will not buy it, and he should not.

### 4.2 The value test that sets the ceiling

A fee is an easy yes when the saving it produces covers it at least three times. Assuming a **realised** saving of two per cent of turnover — deliberately conservative, well below what the analysis typically identifies — the maximum defensible fee is:

| Weekly takings | Monthly turnover | Realised saving at 2% | Maximum fee at 3× cover |
|---|---|---|---|
| £5,500 | £23,832 | £477 | **£159** |
| £7,000 | £30,331 | £607 | **£202** |
| £9,000 | £38,997 | £780 | **£260** |
| £12,000 | £51,996 | £1,040 | **£347** |

This is the single most useful table in the plan. It says the fee must be set by the size of the client, and it says the ceiling for a mid-sized site is around £250 to £350. Version 3's £299 flat rate was above the ceiling for more than half the target market and below it for the rest.

### 4.3 The ladder

| Tier | Price | Who it is for | What they get | Founder hours a month |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Vitals Lite** | £99/mo | Under £7,000 a week | Self-serve tools with saved history, automated weekly cost summary, monthly twenty-minute advisory call | ~1 |
| **Vitals Core** | £249/mo | £7,000–£12,000 a week | Three operating reviews a week from the client's own invoices and till data, weekly variance and procurement note, quarterly menu review | ~4 |
| **Vitals Pro** | £449/mo | Over £12,000 a week, or two sites | Daily review, weekly fifteen-minute call, supplier negotiation support, quarterly strategy session | ~8 |

Effective hourly recovery: £99, £62 and £56 respectively. Lite is the most profitable tier per hour because it is nearly all software. That is not an accident and it should shape where effort goes.

### 4.4 The upsell you identified

You are right that the recurring fee is capped by the client's margin. The money above that cap is in **outcome-linked work**, which the food economist frame licenses and the bookkeeping frame does not:

**Procurement savings share.** Re-source three to five cost lines — fish, oil, packaging, drinks, energy — and take twenty-five per cent of the verified first-year saving, invoiced quarterly in arrears against evidenced invoice prices. A single supplier switch on a fish line at a £9,000-a-week site is commonly worth £3,000 to £6,000 a year. Your share on one line is £750 to £1,500, from perhaps five hours of work, and it costs the client nothing he was not already losing.

This is the highest-value thing in the entire business, it uses your actual expertise rather than your typing speed, and it has no monthly labour attached. Every subscription client should be offered it at month three.

### 4.5 One-off products — cash inside thirty days

These matter because they sell to owners who will not commit to a subscription, and because they convert your knowledge into revenue without a daily obligation.

| Product | Price | Delivery time | Effective rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vitals Workbook — spreadsheet pack, self-serve | £39 | Automatic | £780/hr |
| Kitchen Cost Control course — PDF and video | £79 | Automatic | £1,580/hr |
| Delivery Platform Review — channel contribution analysis | £149 | 2 hours | £74/hr |
| Menu Engineering Report — dish-level GP and mix analysis | £199 | 3 hours | £66/hr |
| Supplier Sourcing Audit — price benchmarking and switch plan | £349 | 5 hours | £70/hr |

The Menu Engineering Report is the sharpest instrument in the range. Restaurant owners buy one-off fixes readily and subscriptions reluctantly. It costs the client less than one evening's covers, it demonstrates exactly what the practice can see, and roughly one in three buyers of a diagnostic will take a retainer within ninety days. Sell the report first and the subscription second.

### 5.1 Underlying assumptions, stated plainly

- Month 1 is a build month with no revenue. This is correct and should not be argued with.
- Subscription additions: one Lite a month from month 2, one Core a month from month 3, one Pro roughly every three months from month 5.

### 5.2 The honest weakness

There is no cash risk in this model. There is a severe **time** risk, and it is set out in the next section. A plan that shows £11,704 in month twelve while requiring 141 delivery hours in that month is not describing a business yet. It is describing a man about to run out of Tuesdays.

## 6. THE REAL CONSTRAINT — AND THE ONLY WAY THROUGH IT

Assume six productive hours a day, twenty-two days a month: 132 hours, of which perhaps sixty per cent can go to delivery, leaving **79 delivery hours a month**.

| Month | Delivery hours required | Position |
|---|---|---|
| 7 | 69 | At the limit |
| 8 | 84 | **Over** |
| 9 | 92 | Over |
| 10 | 110 | Badly over |
| 12 | 141 | Nearly double capacity |

The wall arrives in month eight. Only three responses exist, and two of them are wrong.

**Wrong response one: stop selling.** This caps the business at around £5,000 a month permanently.

**Wrong response two: hire.** At £249 a month against four hours of delivery, a £13-an-hour assistant leaves £197. It works, but it converts a practice into a low-margin agency and it requires cash you do not have.

**The right response: automate the capture, keep the judgement.** Of the four hours a Core client consumes, roughly three are mechanical — reading invoices, keying lines, categorising, formatting the note. One hour is the food economist actually thinking. Software can take the three. It cannot take the one, and it should not try.

| Capture automated | Core hours a month | Effective rate | Core clients one person can carry |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0% (today) | 4.0 | £62/hr | 20 |
| 30% | 2.8 | £89/hr | 28 |
| 50% | 2.0 | £124/hr | 40 |
| 70% | 1.2 | £207/hr | 66 |

**Seventy per cent automation of capture is achievable and it is not achieved with artificial intelligence.** It is achieved with three unglamorous things, all specified in the coding plan:

1. **Supplier templates.** A client buys from the same six suppliers. Each invoice has a fixed layout and largely the same lines week after week. Once the template exists, entry becomes confirmation of quantities rather than transcription.
2. **Recurring-line prediction.** The system proposes last week's lines with this week's quantities pre-filled. The operator corrects the exceptions. This alone removes half the keystrokes with no cleverness at all.
3. **A keyboard-first entry screen.** Not a form. A single screen where an entire invoice is entered without the hand leaving the keyboard, with supplier and category auto-resolved from the first three characters. Eight seconds a line instead of forty.

**Therefore the software is not a month-seven luxury as Version 3 assumed. It is the month-one deliverable**, because your constraint is cash rather than time, and you can build it yourself. That is the central strategic change in this version, and the companion coding plan is its execution.

## 7. THE NINETY-DAY PLAN

### Month 1 — Build, no selling

| Week | Work | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Public tool suite on the existing website: Vitals Check, food cost calculator, delivery commission calculator, labour calculator, waste calculator | Five live pages, indexed |
| 2 | Vitals Workbook built and put on sale; payment link and automated delivery | First revenue line live |
| 3 | Supabase backend, client records, the keyboard-first entry console | Delivery capability exists |
| 4 | Client portal, weekly note generator, Menu Engineering Report template | Full Core tier deliverable |

Nothing is sold in month one and that is deliberate. Selling a service you cannot yet deliver efficiently is how the founder ends up trapped at three clients.

### Month 2 — First revenue

- Publish four articles supporting the tools. Search visibility begins here and takes sixty to ninety days to arrive, so it must start now.
- Approach twelve businesses you can reach without cold walking: existing contacts, church and community networks, suppliers, and the two or three owners who will take a call because of who you are.
- Offer the **Menu Engineering Report at £199** as the opening move. Not the subscription. One-off work opens doors that retainers cannot.
- Target: three reports sold, one Lite subscription, first workbook sales from search.

### Month 3 — Convert diagnostics into retainers

- Every report buyer receives a specific retainer proposal within seven days of delivery, priced by their turnover band from the Section 4.3 ladder.
- Approach the first three suppliers about referral arrangements. A catering butcher or fish merchant speaks to a hundred owners a week and has no conflict with you.
- Offer the first procurement savings share to the strongest Core client.
- Target: £1,200 a month recurring, plus one-off flow.

**Ninety-day target: £1,189 in month three, with four to six active clients and a functioning delivery system.** That is below Version 3's £1,495 target, and it is the more likely number, because it does not assume a ten per cent cold walk-in conversion rate.

## 8. RISKS

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| The free tools do not rank, and the funnel never fills | Medium | High | The tools have standalone sales value in person. Do not rely on search alone: supplier referrals and one-off diagnostics are the primary channel, with search as compounding upside. Review at month four. |
| Clients do not send data reliably | High | High | Lite tier requires nothing from the client at all. For Core and Pro, tie the engagement to a fixed weekly collection point rather than daily submission, and build the automated reminder into the system. |
| Founder time exhausted by month eight | High | Critical | Build the automation in month one, not month seven. This is the plan's central bet. |
| Savings-share disputes over what was actually saved | Medium | Medium | Baseline every re-sourced line in writing before switching, with the invoice price on the date of baselining. Verify against actual invoices, not estimates. Cap the share at twelve months. |
| Positioning drifts back towards bookkeeping under client pressure | Medium | High | Clients will ask you to "just do the books". Decline it and refer it out. The referral relationship with a hospitality bookkeeper is worth more than the fee. |
| A larger competitor enters | Low in year one | Medium | Depth of relationship and genuine food economics expertise are not clonable by a software company. Publish under your own name. |
| Advice acted on causes client loss | Low | High | Professional indemnity insurance, and written advice that states assumptions and gives the client the decision. |

## 9. TERMS OF ENGAGEMENT — REQUIRED CLAUSES

Put these in the engagement letter before the first paid piece of work. They protect the positioning as much as the practice.

**Scope.** "Restaurant Vitals provides food economics and operational consultancy. Our work consists of analysing operating information supplied by the client and advising on cost control, procurement, pricing and operational performance."

**Exclusion.** "We do not provide accountancy, bookkeeping, audit, tax or regulated financial advice, and nothing in our advice should be relied upon as such. The client remains responsible for maintaining its own financial records and for engaging its own accountant and tax adviser."

**Client responsibility.** "Our analysis is based on information supplied by the client. We do not verify or audit that information. Recommendations are commercial advice; decisions to act on them, and their consequences, rest with the client."

**Savings share, where applicable.** "Where a savings share is agreed, the baseline price for each cost line is recorded in writing before any change is made. The share is calculated on verified invoice prices for a period of twelve months from the date of the change and is invoiced quarterly in arrears."

**Liability.** Capped at fees paid in the preceding twelve months.

**Data.** Client operating data is processed to deliver the engagement, retained for the engagement plus twelve months, and not disclosed to third parties. Where a supplier is approached on the client's behalf, only what is necessary is disclosed, and only with consent.
